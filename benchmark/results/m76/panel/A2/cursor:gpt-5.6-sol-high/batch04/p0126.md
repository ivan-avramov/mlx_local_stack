## Response A
# Fine-tune vs. Prompt + Retrieval for a Support Assistant

## The questions to answer before you touch a model

These aren't "nice to know." If you can't answer them, you're guessing at the architecture.

1. **What is the task, precisely?** "Customer support" spans at least four very different problems: retrieving a policy answer, triaging/routing a ticket, walking a user through a multi-step troubleshooting flow, and enforcing a business rule (refund eligibility, escalation thresholds). Retrieval is strong for the first; fine-tuning or structured prompting is stronger for the others. Write down the top five intents you actually see in real tickets and count their share.

2. **What is the cost of a specific failure?** A wrong FAQ answer that a human catches in review is cheap. A wrong refund decision that ships to a customer is a support ticket about the support bot. The failure tolerance determines how much you need to invest in reliability, and reliability is where fine-tuning and RAG diverge.

3. **How does your knowledge change?** If your product ships weekly and your policies, pricing, and troubleshooting docs shift with it, you have a knowledge-freshness problem. This is the single biggest structural argument for retrieval.

4. **What data do you actually have?** Do you have 200 historical transcripts or 200,000? Do they have clean labels, or are they noisy and full of human agents who also get things wrong? Fine-tuning on bad labels teaches the model to be confidently wrong.

5. **What is your volume and latency budget?** 40 tickets a day through a $20/month API is a rounding error. 40,000/day at 200 tokens each is a real line item, and self-hosting an open-weight model starts to matter. Real-time chat (< 2 s first token) vs. async ticket triage changes the latency math.

6. **What can your team actually maintain?** A fine-tuned model is a pipeline: data curation, training job, eval harness, retraining cadence, rollback, and a person who understands why the eval regressed after Tuesday's retrain. If you're a two-person team, that's a second product you're building.

7. **Do you need auditability or compliance?** "Why did the bot say that?" is a real question when a customer disputes a refund. Retrieval gives you a citation to a document. A fine-tuned model gives you a probability distribution.

## The experiments that actually discriminate

The mistake most teams make is deciding the architecture before they have evidence. Here's a sequence that forces the question to answer itself.

**Step 1 — Build the retrieval baseline and measure it honestly.**
Chunk your knowledge base, embed it, build a retrieval-augmented prompt, and evaluate on a held-out set of 100–300 real support interactions (not synthetic prompts). Score each on: did it retrieve the right document, did it answer correctly, did it hallucinate a policy that doesn't exist. You now have a number.

**Step 2 — Catalog the failure modes by category.**
Break the wrong answers into: *retrieval failures* (right answer exists in the docs, bot didn't find it), *reasoning failures* (right docs retrieved, bot drew the wrong conclusion), *knowledge gaps* (the answer isn't in any document), and *behavior/format failures* (right content, wrong tone, wrong structure, didn't follow the escalation rule). This categorization is the whole decision.

**Step 3 — Fine-tune on a small, curated set and compare on the same eval.**
Take 500–2,000 of your best-labeled examples. Fine-tune a mid-size open-weight model (Llama 3 8B, Mistral 7B, Qwen 7B — anything you can self-host). Evaluate on the *same* held-out set. Now you have two numbers on the same yardstick.

**Step 4 — Test the things that matter operationally, not just accuracy.**
- **Knowledge staleness:** Update one policy document. Does the RAG system reflect it immediately? Does the fine-tuned model still answer with the old policy? (It will, until you retrain.)
- **Long tail:** Hold out the 10% of intents that are rare. Does fine-tuning help there, or did it just memorize the head?
- **Multi-turn consistency:** Run a 6-turn troubleshooting conversation. Does the fine-tuned model stay in the right "mode"? Does the RAG system re-retrieve sensibly each turn?
- **Latency and cost at your real volume:** Measure tokens per interaction, retrieval latency, and inference cost for both.

**Step 5 — Test the hybrid.**
Fine-tune for behavior and format (tone, escalation rules, output structure, refusal patterns) and use retrieval for factual content. Evaluate this as a third option. In my experience this is where most support assistants actually land, and it's the answer that makes the binary question a false dichotomy.

## The cost model

Don't just compare API tokens. The real cost structure has four layers:

| | Prompt + RAG | Fine-tuned open-weight |
|---|---|---|
| **Upfront engineering** | Chunking, embedding, vector store, prompt iteration. Days to a couple weeks. | Data curation, training pipeline, eval harness, self-hosting infra. Weeks to months. |
| **Per-interaction inference** | API call (or self-hosted) + embedding lookup. Cheap at low volume. | Self-hosted GPU or API. Cheaper per token at high volume, but you own the infra. |
| **Ongoing maintenance** | Update the docs, re-embed. Hours. | Retrain on new data, re-eval, redeploy. Days, and someone has to babysit it. |
| **Knowledge freshness** | Automatic. | Stale until retrained. This is the hidden cost. |
| **Engineering headcount** | A competent prompt engineer + retrieval tuning. | An ML engineer who understands training dynamics and eval design. |

At 500 interactions/day, the per-token difference is negligible and the decision is entirely about engineering time and maintenance burden. At 50,000/day, self-hosting an open-weight model cuts inference cost by an order of magnitude and the fine-tuning investment amortizes. The crossover is usually somewhere around 5,000–10,000 interactions/day, but it depends heavily on your token length and whether you're already paying for GPU infra.

## When each answer is right

**Retrieval + prompting is the right first move when:**
- The dominant task is "answer from our documents" (FAQs, policies, product docs, troubleshooting guides).
- Your knowledge changes more often than you can retrain.
- You're a small team and need to ship in weeks, not months.
- You need to show the customer *which document* the answer came from.
- Your volume is below the self-hosting crossover.
- You haven't yet proven that prompting + retrieval is insufficient. **This last one is the most important condition. Don't fine-tune until you've measured the gap.**

**Fine-tuning is justified when:**
- You have a large, stable corpus of well-labeled examples (10k+ clean interactions) and the task involves reasoning patterns, not just fact lookup — e.g., diagnosing a bug from symptoms, applying a multi-condition eligibility rule.
- You need a specific output format, brand voice, or behavioral constraint that prompting can't reliably enforce (the model keeps adding disclaimers, or keeps refusing things it shouldn't).
- You're at high volume and self-hosting, so the marginal cost of fine-tuning is just training compute, not a per-token API premium.
- You need sub-second latency and can't afford the retrieval round-trip in the hot path.
- The task is closer to classification or structured extraction than open-ended QA.

**The hybrid is right when:**
- You need retrieval for factual accuracy *and* fine-tuning for behavior. This is the most common real-world answer. Fine-tune a small model on format and tone; retrieve for content. You get the freshness of RAG with the behavioral control of fine-tuning, and the fine-tuning set is small and stable (behavior doesn't change weekly the way product docs do).

## The decision in one paragraph

Build the retrieval baseline first, evaluate it on real interactions, and categorize the failures. If the failures are mostly retrieval and knowledge gaps, better retrieval is your fix — spend your engineering time there. If the failures are behavioral and you have the labeled data, fine-tune for behavior and keep retrieval for facts. If your volume is low and your team is small, the maintenance burden of a fine-tuned pipeline is the real cost, and a well-tuned RAG system will get you 90% of the way with a fraction of the ongoing effort. The question isn't "which is better?" It's "what is the specific, measured gap that only fine-tuning closes, and can I afford to maintain it?"

## Response B
# Fine-tune vs. Prompt + Retrieval: A Decision Framework

## 1. Questions to Answer Before Touching Either Option

These are the questions that actually change the answer. Get them wrong and you'll build the wrong system and rationalize it.

**About the task itself**
- Is the assistant's job primarily *knowledge retrieval* ("what's your refund policy?"), *structured extraction* (pull order ID, issue category from a messy ticket), *behavioral* (multi-turn clarification, de-escalation, knowing when to hand off to a human), or *generation* (draft a reply in the company's voice)? Each of these has a different optimal architecture. Knowledge retrieval favors RAG. Behavioral and stylistic demands favor fine-tuning. Extraction can go either way.
- How much of the "right answer" lives in documents you can point the model at versus in *how* the model should behave? If a support rep's value is mostly "here's the policy, here's the empathy, here's the escalation," the knowledge part is retrievable and the behavior part is a training problem.

**About your data**
- How many *labeled* examples of good support interactions do you have? "We have 40,000 past tickets" is not the same as "we have 4,000 tickets with a gold-standard reply and a quality score." The fine-tuning decision hinges on having a few hundred to a few thousand *curated* examples, not raw volume.
- Is your knowledge base stable or churning? If the product ships weekly and the docs change with it, RAG absorbs that for free. A fine-tuned model bakes in last month's pricing tiers and you have a silent regression.

**About constraints**
- Latency budget, concurrency, and cost-per-interaction. Are you serving 50 tickets/day or 50,000?
- Data residency / compliance. If PII can't leave your VPC, open-weights-on-your-own-infra is a hard requirement, not a preference. This single constraint can override everything else.
- Team shape. Do you have an ML engineer who will own retraining, eval pipelines, and drift monitoring? Or a two-person team that needs something that works and doesn't need babysitting?

**About the baseline**
- What does the *current* experience look like? A human agent? A rules-based bot? The gap you need to close matters more than the absolute capability of either option.

## 2. The Experiments That Discriminate

The single most important move: **build the RAG baseline first, measure it, and only then ask whether fine-tuning closes a gap that prompting can't.** Most startups skip this and fine-tune into a problem that a good retrieval pipeline would have solved.

Run these on the same held-out set of 200–500 real tickets (stratified by issue type, complexity, and whether they required escalation):

| Experiment | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **A. Strong-prompt baseline.** Base model, well-crafted system prompt, no retrieval. | The floor. How much of the task is the model already good at? |
| **B. RAG baseline.** Same prompt + retrieval over your docs, policies, past tickets. Tune chunking, top-k, reranking. | Whether the knowledge gap is the bottleneck. If B >> A, retrieval is doing the heavy lifting. |
| **C. Fine-tune on curated examples, no retrieval.** | Whether the model needs to *learn behavior* (tone, format, escalation heuristics) that prompting can't express. |
| **D. Fine-tune + RAG.** | The ceiling. Is there a compounding effect, or does fine-tuning on top of RAG add noise? |

Score each on the metrics that matter to you—resolution rate, hallucination rate (did it cite a policy that doesn't exist?), format compliance, escalation accuracy, and a human-rated quality score on a sample.

The discriminating signal is the **B → D delta**. If B is already at 90% of D, you don't need fine-tuning. If B plateaus at 70% and D reaches 90%, the gap is behavioral and fine-tuning is earning its keep.

One more experiment that's cheap and revealing: take 50 tickets where the RAG system gave a *factually correct but stylistically wrong* answer (too formal, wrong format, failed to ask a clarifying question). If prompting can fix those with a better system prompt and few-shot examples, you don't need fine-tuning for style. If it can't, the behavior is genuinely baked in and you need training.

## 3. The Cost Model

This is where startups get surprised. The obvious cost is inference. The real cost is the system.

**Prompt + RAG (typically on a hosted API or self-hosted open weights):**
- Prompt engineering and evaluation: a few engineer-weeks, then ongoing iteration as the product and docs change.
- Retrieval infra: embedding pipeline, vector store, reranker. At startup scale this is a managed service (Pinecone, Weaviate, or even pgvector) and costs hundreds of dollars/month.
- Inference: API calls. At $2–$12 per 1M tokens depending on model, a support interaction that uses ~2K input + 500 output tokens is a few cents. At 10K interactions/day that's $100–$300/day. At 100K/day it starts to hurt.
- Maintenance: mostly prompt updates and retrieval tuning when docs change. Low ongoing ML burden.
- **Hidden cost:** prompt fragility. A model provider updates their model and your carefully tuned prompts regress. You need eval regression tests.

**Fine-tuning an open-weight model:**
- Data curation: this is the real cost. 1,000–5,000 high-quality examples, hand-labeled or carefully filtered. At $10–$50 per labeled example with contractors, that's $10K–$250K up front, plus the engineering time to build the labeling pipeline.
- Training compute: LoRA/QLoRA fine-tuning on a 7B–70B model is a few hours on an A100/H100, roughly $100–$500 per run. Cheap in absolute terms.
- **Serving infra:** this is the big one. You now own the GPU. A 7B model at reasonable throughput needs ~$200–$500/month per GPU. A 70B model needs a multi-GPU box, $2K–$10K/month. At low volume this is more expensive than API calls. At high volume (tens of thousands of interactions/day) it inverts and becomes cheaper.
- Retraining cadence: every time the knowledge or desired behavior shifts, you retrain. Budget an engineer's time per cycle.
- **Hidden cost:** you now own the model's failure modes. A fine-tuned model that confidently answers wrong is harder to debug than a RAG system where you can inspect the retrieved chunks. You need eval infrastructure, drift monitoring, and a rollback story.

**The crossover point** is usually around 20K–50K interactions/day for a 7B model, lower for 70B. Below that, API inference is cheaper and you don't need to babysit GPUs.

## 4. Conditions Under Which Each Answer Is Right

**Prompting + RAG is the right answer when:**
- The task is dominated by *knowledge*: "what's the policy, what's the price, what's the troubleshooting step." The docs are the source of truth and they change often.
- You have fewer than ~500 curated training examples, or your "training data" is unstructured past tickets with no gold labels.
- Your team is small and doesn't have a dedicated ML engineer for retraining and eval maintenance.
- Your interaction volume is below the GPU-serving breakeven.
- You need to iterate fast on product changes and can't afford a retraining cycle every time the pricing page changes.
- You're early and need to validate the concept. A good RAG system is a weekend project; a fine-tuned model is a quarter project.

**Fine-tuning is the right answer when:**
- The bottleneck is *behavior, not knowledge*: the model retrieves the right policy but writes a reply that's too terse, misses the escalation cue, or formats the output wrong. Prompting has hit its ceiling.
- You have (or can afford to create) 1,000+ high-quality examples of the exact interaction pattern you want.
- You're at high enough volume that self-hosting open weights is cheaper than API calls, *and* you have the infra maturity to run it.
- Compliance requires data to never leave your infrastructure.
- You need a specific output format (structured JSON with particular fields, a fixed reply template) that the base model resists despite prompting.
- The "style" is a core brand differentiator and few-shot prompting can't capture it reliably.

**The honest answer for most startups:** start with RAG. It's faster to build, easier to debug, absorbs knowledge changes, and gets you 70–85% of the way. Then evaluate the residual gap. Add fine-tuning *only* for the specific behavioral gap that retrieval and prompting can't close, and scope it narrowly (fine-tune for tone and format, keep retrieval for facts). The combined system—fine-tuned model + RAG—is often the end state, but you should arrive there empirically, not by default.

The failure mode to avoid is treating fine-tuning as a prestige choice. "We fine-tuned Llama" is a great slide. "Our retrieval pipeline has a 94% answer-correctness rate and a 12% hallucination rate" is a better business outcome, and it's what you can actually maintain.
