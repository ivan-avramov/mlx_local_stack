# Review: A/B Test of New Ranking Model — 0.8% Lift, p = 0.03, 3 Weeks

**Verdict: Not shippable yet. Extend the test and run the analyses below. I'll revisit after.**

---

## What I'd need to believe before trusting this number

**The effect is small and the significance is thin.** A 0.8% relative lift with a p-value of 0.03 sits right at the boundary where a real effect and a false positive are hard to distinguish. I need to see the confidence interval, not just the p-value. If the 95% CI is something like [0.1%, 1.5%], the lower bound is barely off zero and the practical decision is "maybe." If it's [0.4%, 1.2%], I'm more comfortable. Show me the interval.

**What was the power calculation?** Before the test started, what was the minimum detectable effect at 80% power for the chosen sample size? If the MDE was 1.5%, then a 0.8% result is *below* the powered threshold and the p-value of 0.03 is essentially a coin-flip artifact of the particular sample you drew. If the MDE was 0.5%, we're in better shape. I want the original test plan, not a post-hoc justification.

**Was the metric pre-registered?** What was the single primary metric, decided before launch? If the team looked at 15 metrics and reported the one that crossed 0.05, the effective p-value is much worse than 0.03. I need the full metric table with all the nulls.

**Sample ratio mismatch.** Did the traffic split hold at ~50/50 (or whatever the design was)? A 0.3% SRM is a red flag that something in the assignment or logging pipeline is broken, and it can produce spurious significance. Check it.

## What would make me distrust it

- **Peeking.** If anyone looked at the metric on day 7, day 10, day 14, and day 21 and stopped when it crossed significance, the p-value is inflated. I need to know the stopping rule. If there was no pre-committed stopping rule and they just "checked it when it looked good," I'm discounting the result heavily.
- **Novelty / primacy effect.** Three weeks is the bare minimum to wash out novelty for a ranking change. Users who see a reordered feed get a curiosity bump in the first few days that decays. Plot the daily metric by cohort-day-since-assignment. If the lift is front-loaded and decaying, the steady-state effect is smaller than 0.8% and the test is still running.
- **Weekly seasonality.** Three weeks = three weekly cycles. That's enough to see a weekly pattern but not enough to be confident you've averaged over it, especially if the test launched on a Monday and you're comparing against a baseline that includes a different holiday or event. I want to see the day-of-week decomposition.
- **Interference / spillover.** A ranking model change can affect the supply side (creators, sellers, publishers) or create network effects. If the randomization unit is the user but the treatment changes what content is shown to everyone, the effective sample size is smaller than the nominal one and the p-value is optimistic. What's the randomization unit and is it valid for this change?
- **Ratio-metric pitfalls.** If the metric is a ratio (CTR, conversion rate, revenue per session), the variance estimator matters. A naive z-test on a ratio can understate variance. Did they use a delta-method or bootstrap CI?

## Analyses I'm demanding before a ship decision

1. **Metric trajectory plot**, daily, for both arms, with the full 21 days. I want to see whether the lift is stable, growing, or decaying.
2. **Segment breakdown.** Does the 0.8% hold across new vs. returning users, platform (iOS/Android/desktop), geography, and engagement tier? A lift that exists only in one segment is a different decision than a uniform lift.
3. **Guardrail metrics.** Did anything else move? Latency, diversity of results, long-term retention proxy, complaint rate, session length. A ranking model that lifts CTR by 0.8% while cratering result diversity or long-term engagement is a regression.
4. **Position-level analysis.** For a ranking model, I want to know *where* the change happened. Did it reorder the top 3, or is it shuffling positions 8–20? The mechanism matters for whether the effect is durable.
5. **Full metric table with all tested metrics and their p-values**, so I can assess the multiple-comparisons situation honestly.
6. **SRM check and a sanity check on the logging pipeline** for both arms.

## How I'd decide whether to ship

This is a judgment call, not a binary gate, but here's my rubric:

- **If the CI lower bound is above ~0.3–0.4%, the trajectory is flat (not decaying), SRM is clean, guardrails are neutral or positive, and the lift is consistent across segments:** ship it, but with a post-launch monitoring window of 2–4 weeks on the same metric and guardrails. The effect is real but small enough that I want to confirm it holds in production traffic rather than the test harness.

- **If the trajectory shows decay, the CI lower bound is near zero, or the lift concentrates in one segment:** extend the test by another 2–3 weeks. A 0.8% lift that's still settling isn't a ship decision; it's a "wait and re-evaluate" decision.

- **If there's an SRM issue, evidence of peeking, or a guardrail that moved adversely:** don't ship. Investigate the root cause first.

- **If the only evidence is "p = 0.03" with no CI, no trajectory, no segments, no guardrails:** that's not a result, that's a number someone pulled off a dashboard. Send it back.

The core principle: a 0.8% lift is a small bet. The cost of shipping a false positive is a ranking model change that's expensive to roll back and that quietly degrades user experience in ways the primary metric won't catch. The cost of waiting two more weeks is low. The asymmetry favors caution here.

---

*Bottom line: I don't distrust the team or the methodology in principle, but I don't have enough evidence yet to call this a real effect. Run the analyses above, extend if the trajectory isn't flat, and bring it back. I'm happy to re-review within a week.*

## Response B
# Review: Proposed A/B Test — New Ranking Model

## Bottom line

A 0.8% lift at p = 0.03 after three weeks is a *plausible* result, not a *convincing* one. I would not ship on this evidence as presented. Below is what I need to see before I change my mind.

---

## What would make me trust this number

- **The metric is the one that matters.** 0.8% of what? If it's a guardrail-adjacent metric like "clicks per session" and the north-star metric (retention, long-term engagement, revenue) moved 0.1% with p = 0.41, the headline is misleading. I need to know which metric the p-value refers to and whether it's the metric the ranking model was actually designed to optimize.
- **The confidence interval is tight enough to be actionable.** p = 0.03 tells me the effect is probably not zero. It does not tell me the effect is 0.8%. I want the 95% CI. If it's [0.02%, 1.58%], the lower bound is so close to zero that the practical lift could be negligible. If it's [0.3%, 1.3%], I'm more comfortable.
- **The test was adequately powered.** I need to see the pre-test power analysis or, at minimum, the achieved sample size and observed power. A test that was underpowered and "happened to cross 0.05" by luck is a coin flip that landed heads.
- **The randomization is clean.** No obvious bucket-imbalance issues, no users leaking between arms, no bot traffic inflating one arm. I want to see the SRM (sample ratio mismatch) check.
- **The effect is stable across the three weeks.** I want a week-by-week breakdown. If week 1 shows +1.4% and week 3 shows +0.2%, that's a novelty effect or a decaying effect, and the "0.8%" average is a misleading summary. A flat line across all three weeks is what I want to see.

## What would make me distrust it

- **They peeked.** If anyone looked at the metric daily and stopped the test the first day it crossed significance, the p-value is meaningless. I need to know the stopping rule. Sequential testing (always-valid p-values, mSPRT) or a fixed-horizon design are the only ones I'll accept.
- **Multiple metrics were tested and this is the best one.** If the team ran 15 metrics and reported the one that crossed 0.05, the effective p-value is closer to 0.30 after a Bonferroni or FDR correction. I need to know the full set of metrics they looked at.
- **The test window overlaps a known external event.** A product launch, a holiday, a competitor action, a data pipeline incident — any of these can create a spurious differential. I want the test window checked against the incident calendar.
- **The effect is concentrated in one segment.** If +0.8% is driven entirely by, say, iOS users in one region, and other segments show zero or negative effects, the "average" is not representative of the population I'd be shipping to.

## Additional analyses I would demand before a decision

1. **Segment-level breakdown.** New vs. returning users. Top-tier vs. long-tail content. By platform. By geography. By user tenure. If the effect is homogeneous, good. If it's heterogeneous, I need to understand *why* before I extrapolate the average to the full population.

2. **Guardrail and secondary metrics.** At minimum: retention (7-day, 30-day), session length, diversity of content consumed, negative-feedback rate (dislikes, "not interested" taps), and any latency or error-rate metrics for the new model. A ranking model that lifts clicks by 0.8% by showing more sensational content and cratering diversity is not a win.

3. **Effect trajectory over time.** Plot the daily or weekly treatment-vs-control difference. I want to see whether the effect is stable, growing, or decaying. Three weeks is the *minimum* to rule out a novelty effect for a ranking change, not a guarantee. If the curve is still trending down, I'd want another two weeks.

4. **Absolute impact, not just relative.** 0.8% of what base? On a metric with a base of 2%, that's 0.016 percentage points. On a metric with a base of 40%, that's 0.32 points. The absolute number matters for the cost-benefit calculation.

5. **Cost and risk of the new model.** What's the added inference latency? Compute cost? Engineering complexity? Model drift risk? If the new model costs 3× the serving infrastructure of the current one, 0.8% has to clear a higher bar than if it's a drop-in with the same latency.

6. **Heterogeneous treatment effects / CATE analysis.** Even a quick analysis: does the effect differ by user engagement tier? If heavy users see no lift and light users see +2%, that changes the rollout strategy (and the risk profile).

## How I would decide whether to ship

I'd frame it as a decision under uncertainty, not a binary "significant / not significant" call:

- **If the CI lower bound is meaningfully positive, guardrails are clean, the effect is stable over three weeks, and the model's cost/risk is modest:** ship. A 0.8% lift on a core engagement metric is real and worth taking. Don't wait for p < 0.01.

- **If the CI lower bound is near zero, the effect is decaying week over week, or any guardrail is trending negative:** do not ship. Run another 2–3 weeks. The expected value of waiting is high relative to the cost of a bad ship.

- **If the effect is concentrated in one segment or one user type:** consider a partial rollout or a targeted rollout to that segment, with a plan to extend if the effect generalizes.

- **If the test was peeked, underpowered, or the metric is a proxy for something I care about more:** the result is not decision-grade. Redo the test with a pre-registered design, a fixed stopping rule, and the right primary metric.

- **If the cost of the new model is high (latency, infra, maintenance):** I'd want to see a cost-adjusted estimate. 0.8% lift minus the engineering and infra cost, annualized, is the number that matters. If it's roughly break-even, the risk of a subtle long-term negative effect (filter bubbles, diversity erosion) tips the balance toward not shipping.

---

**TL;DR:** The result is in the "interesting, needs more evidence" zone. I'd ask for the CI, the segment breakdown, the week-by-week trajectory, the full guardrail picture, and confirmation of a clean test design. If all of those check out, ship. If any of them are shaky, extend the test by two weeks and re-evaluate. I would not approve a ship on p = 0.03 and a single aggregate metric alone.
